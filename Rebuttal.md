**Reviewer CueK**
1. **Limited Novelty**:  We agree that topology-aware graph discrimination with GNNs, including GIN-based approaches, is well studied. Our contribution is not another GIN modification to increase local message-passing capacity; rather, we introduce UTS, **which provides global topological information inaccessible to 1-WL-bounded neighborhood aggregation**. For example, $C_6$ and $2\times C_3$ (Figure 1) are 1-WL-equivalent and therefore indistinguishable by standard message passing, despite having different global cycle and connectivity structures. In contrast, Graph-UTS ($\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$), computed directly from the input graph using persistent topology and complementary geometric/spectral information, assigns distinct signatures to these graphs. *Such information cannot be obtained by increasing the depth or width of a GIN while remaining within the 1-WL framework*.
We evaluate UTS across three modular interventions incorporating it into representation, optimization, and pooling. *The unmodified and unregularized GIN baselines use the same architecture, so improvements from these interventions are not due to additional architectural capacity*. Experiments across MUTAG, PROTEINS, and COLLAB show that these interventions are not tied to a specific dataset or graph domain.

2. **Is the cause of improvement really UTS or an additional number of parameters?** To disentangle the contribution of UTS from increased representation capacity, we conducted a capacity-controlled experiment with identical descriptor dimensionality and architecture. We replace the UTS descriptors with Gaussian random vectors of identical dimensionality. **Random Graph-UTS** replaces only the static $\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$ with a random vector of the same dimensionality, while retaining the learnable $\Phi_{\mathrm{emb}}$. This control isolates the contribution of the static topological signal and rules out the possibility that the performance difference under Random Dual-UTS is caused by replacing the learnable Embedding-UTS with a static random vector. **Random Dual-UTS** replaces both $\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{emb}}$ with random vectors of the corresponding dimensions, providing a full capacity-matched control. Complete details and results can be found in Appendix B.3
3. **Experiments on larger datasets:** We agree that the current evaluation on three TU datasets provides limited evidence for broader generalization. We are extending the evaluation to OGBG-PPA, which contains 158,100 graphs—approximately $32\times$ more graphs than COLLAB—with an average of 243.4 nodes and 2,266.1 edges per graph. Due to computational constraints, these experiments are ongoing and will be included in the camera-ready version.
4. **Scalability:**  We conducted runtime analysis to measure per-graph UTS computation time as a function of graph size, using a synthetic dataset with a fixed average degree of 4 per graph and up to approximately 1,000 nodes. The cost varies substantially across signature variants. At approximately 1,000 nodes, Embedding-UTS and the Lightweight Graph-UTS variant (§4.4) require approximately 0.75 s and 0.38 s per graph, respectively, compared with approximately $\sim4$ s for the full Graph-UTS. Figure 6 in section G.2 of appendix provides a complete summary. The results show that the lightweight and embedding-based variants remain practical as graph size increases, while substantially improving upon the scaling behavior of the full signature.

10. **Theoretical Tension:** We thank the reviewer for raising this point, as it helps us in further clarifying our pipeline. On closer inspection, the "disabled for cost reasons" characterization applies to only one specific component of our framework — the differentiable training-time surrogate used inside the topo-evolution loss, which caps H1 (cycle) computation above 50 nodes for training-loop tractability (every forward pass, every graph, every epoch). It does not apply to the Embedding-UTS descriptor used in UTS-Aug, which computes full cycle-aware (H1) persistence for graphs of any size, with no node-count cutoff - including PROTEINS' largest graphs at 620 nodes.

This distinction matters because the descriptor is computed once, as a precomputed input feature, not recomputed at every training step. Our scalability benchmark (Figure 6) directly quantifies this: exact Embedding-UTS costs approximately 0.3 seconds at n=620 — trivial as a one-time pass over the dataset, even though the same cost would be prohibitive if paid every forward pass during training, which is precisely why the differentiable surrogate (a fundamentally different computational context) uses the cap instead.

Critically, this means the paper's strongest empirical result — Dual-UTS achieving the best accuracy on both PROTEINS and COLLAB (Table 1) — is achieved with full cycle-structure information present for every graph in both datasets, including the largest ones. The theoretical motivation is therefore not merely illustrated on graphs where the computation is disabled; it is empirically validated on exactly the graphs the reviewer is concerned about, via the one pathway (UTS-Aug) where no cap exists.

The cap genuinely does apply to the topo-evolution loss, and we do not dispute that here the training-time surrogate loses cycle sensitivity above 50 nodes — but this is a distinct, separately-motivated design tradeoff (training tractability vs. one-time feature computation), not evidence that the theoretical contribution as a whole is untested where it matters.
