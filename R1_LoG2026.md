1. **Limited novelty:** We agree that topology-aware graph discrimination with GNNs, including GIN-based approaches, is well studied. However, our contribution is not another architectural modification of GIN to increase the capacity of local message passing; rather, we introduce **UTS, which provides global topological information that is inaccessible to 1-WL-bounded neighborhood aggregation**, enabling it to distinguish graph pairs that standard message-passing GNNs cannot. For instance, $C_6$ and $2\times C_3$ (in Figure 1 of the paper) are 1-WL-equivalent graphs and produce identical representations, thus remaining indistinguishable under standard message passing despite having different global cycle and connectivity structures. In contrast, the Graph-UTS ($\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$) signature, computed directly from the input graph via persistent topology and complementary geometric/spectral information, assigns distinct signatures to the two graphs. This information cannot be obtained by increasing the depth or width of the GIN backbone while remaining within the 1-WL message-passing framework.

More importantly, **UTS is not introduced as a single GIN augmentation or a dataset-specific feature engineering technique.** We study *where global topology can be incorporated into GNN learning* through three modular and independently pluggable interventions, as shown in Figure 2 of the paper:

- **UTS-Aug** injects global topological information into the graph representation through Graph-UTS;
- **UTS-Reg** uses topology as a training-time constraint, regularizing the evolution of the learned embedding space; and
- **UTS-Pool** uses local topological information to guide graph coarsening.

This decomposition is important because the same topological information is shown to be useful at the **representation, optimization, and graph-coarsening stages**, rather than being tied to a particular GIN architecture. The compared baselines, i.e., unmodified and unregularized GIN, use the same architecture; only the representation or training objective is modified. Thus, the observed improvements **cannot** be attributed to additional architectural capacity or parameters, but to the incorporation of UTS. The experiments further span molecular (MUTAG), biological (PROTEINS), and social-network (COLLAB) graph domains, demonstrating that the framework is not designed around a single dataset or graph type.

2. **Is the cause of improvement really UTS or an additional number of parameters?** 
We thank the reviewer for raising this important concern. To disentangle the contribution of UTS from increased representation capacity, we conducted a dedicated capacity-controlled experiment with identical descriptor dimensionality and architecture. We replace the UTS descriptors with Gaussian random vectors of identical dimensionality, while keeping the architecture and parameterization unchanged.  Thus, the random-vector controls provide the same additional representation dimensionality without the topological signal. 

Specifically, we consider two controls to isolate the contributions of the two signatures. **Random Graph-UTS** replaces only the static $\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$ with a random vector of the same dimensionality, while retaining the learnable $\Phi_{\mathrm{emb}}$. This control isolates the contribution of the static topological signal and rules out the possibility that the performance difference under Random Dual-UTS is caused by replacing the learnable Embedding-UTS with a static random vector. **Random Dual-UTS** replaces both $\Phi_{\mathrm{grph}}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{emb}}$ with random vectors of the corresponding dimensions, providing a full capacity-matched control.

On **PROTEINS**, both capacity-controlled comparisons show statistically significant improvements. For **Dual-UTS**, the paired difference relative to its matched random control is **$+0.0127$** (95% CI: **$[+0.0029,+0.0226]$**), with a paired $t$-test of *p* = **0.0197** and bootstrap *p* = **0.0100**. For **Graph-UTS**, the corresponding paired difference is **$+0.0230$** (95% CI: **$[+0.0048,+0.0412]$**), with paired $t$-test *p* = **0.0146** and bootstrap *p* = **0.0180**. Thus, both real UTS variants outperform random vectors of identical dimensionality, indicating that the gains are attributable to the topological signal rather than representation dimensionality alone.

On **MUTAG**, both effects are directionally positive but not statistically significant. **Dual-UTS** shows a paired difference of **$+0.0540$** (95% CI: **$[-0.0112,+0.1192]$**, paired $t$-test *p* = **0.1036**; bootstrap *p* = **0.1089**), while **Graph-UTS** shows a paired difference of **$+0.0110$** (95% CI: **$[-0.0068,+0.0288]$**, paired $t$-test *p* = **0.2254**; bootstrap *p* = **0.2210**). Given the relatively small dataset size of 188 graphs and the resulting variability, these comparisons have limited statistical power; we therefore make no significance claim for MUTAG.

On **COLLAB**, both capacity-controlled comparisons are statistically significant. **Dual-UTS** achieves a paired difference of **$+0.0125$** over its matched random control (95% CI: **$[+0.0043,+0.0207]$**, paired $t$-test *p* = **0.0012**), while **Graph-UTS** achieves a larger paired difference of **$+0.0250$** (95% CI: **$[+0.0178,+0.0322]$**, paired $t$-test *p* = **0.0006**). Thus, real UTS representations significantly outperform matched random vectors of identical dimensionality on COLLAB as well.

Taken together, these capacity-controlled experiments provide direct evidence that the gains from both **Dual-UTS** and **Graph-UTS** arise from their **topological signal rather than simply from increased representation dimensionality**. The effect is statistically significant for both variants on PROTEINS and COLLAB, while remaining directionally positive but inconclusive on MUTAG.

3. **Experiments on larger datasets:** We agree that the current evaluation on three TU datasets: MUTAG, PROTEINS, and COLLAB provides limited evidence for broader generalization. To extend the evaluation beyond the currently largest benchmark, COLLAB (which contains 5,000 graphs), we are conducting additional experiments on OGBG-PPA, a substantially larger graph-classification dataset containing 158,100 graphs, approximately $32 \times$ as many graphs as COLLAB, with an average of 243.4 nodes and 2,266.1 edges per graph. Due to computational and time constraints, these experiments are currently still running, and the resulting evaluation will be included in the camera-ready version.

4. **Scalability:** We conducted an additional runtime analysis by measuring per-graph UTS computation time as a function of graph size (up to ∼ ⁣1,000 nodes).  The results show that the computational cost depends substantially on the signature variant. The exact Embedding-UTS and, particularly, the lightweight variant of Graph-UTS (explained in section 4.4) remain considerably cheaper than the original Graph-UTS. At ∼ ⁣1,000 nodes, Emb-UTS and Lightweight Graph-UTS per-graph wall-clock times are approximately 0.75 sec and 0.38 sec, respectively, compared with ∼ ⁣4 sec for Graph-UTS. Disabling the Ricci-curvature component further reduces the Graph-UTS cost relative to the full signature. These results indicate that while the full Graph-UTS becomes increasingly expensive on larger graphs, the lightweight and embedding-based variants provide substantially more practical alternatives for scaling UTS to larger graphs.
